\section*{Abstract}


This work draws on network theory, economic sociology and ecology to analyse U.S. venture‐capital syndication patterns, once understanding how venture capitalists organise and collaborate in funding new ventures requires moving beyond firm–level analysis to examine the large–scale structure of co‑investment networks. 

Using more than 100k investment records from Crunchbase (covering around 16k investors and 11k companies) we construct a bipartite network linking early–stage and late–stage investors through their shared funding rounds. Community detection and nestedness analysis are applied to identify structural heterogeneity and hierarchical patterns in these networks.

The analysis reveals three major investor communities with power‑law degree distributions.  One community (largely centred in Silicon Valley) exhibits significantly high nestedness: less‑connected investors (specialists) co‐invest almost with subsets of the partners of highly connected investors (generalists), like in ecology's pollinator-plant networks. 

Temporal analysis shows that this hierarchical structure emerges abruptly around 2019, when network connectance crosses a critical threshold, and persists thereafter. By contrast, similarly sized communities without nested organisation display lower transaction volumes and lack geographic concentration.

These findings suggest that network topology, rather than community size or investor centrality alone, influences investment efficiency and access to capital.  By integrating concepts from finance, social network theory and ecology, the thesis contributes a novel framework for analysing innovation ecosystems and opens avenues for predicting investment patterns and designing policies to foster venture‐capital markets behavior.

\textbf{Keywords}: \textit{venture capital, syndication networks, nestedness, network theory, investor communities, Silicon Valley, innovation ecosystems, bipartite networks, community detection, critical transitions}
